Noise and Interference in Instrumentation, Part 1: Introduction

This first lecture covers the basics that Part 2 builds on: how to represent noise sources in a circuit, how to quantify them, when they can simply be added together, how they pass through a frequency-dependent system, and how to compare a system's own noise against a standard reference.

1. Where noise comes from

  • Inherent (transmitted) noise: noise that is already present in the physical quantity the sensor measures, before the signal reaches the instrument.
  • Intrinsic noise: noise generated inside the instrument itself. Every practical component adds noise to the signal it carries (e.g. thermal noise in a resistor, shot noise in a diode).
  • Interference: noise coupled in from external sources that have no connection to the signal of interest (e.g. powerline hum, solar flares, lightning). It is separate from intrinsic noise, although how much of it couples in depends on the system's design.

Modeling noise this way, instead of treating it as one unstructured nuisance, makes design optimization possible: better source/load matching, component choice, filtering and bandwidth decisions, and a realistic uncertainty floor for measurements.

2. Modeling a noisy device

For a simple two-terminal (1-port) device, such as a resistor, a non-ideal capacitor or inductor, a diode or a battery, the noise it generates can be modeled with a single source added to an otherwise ideal, noiseless version of the device:

Fig. 1: A noisy 1-port device modelled as a Thevenin (series voltage) or Norton (parallel current) equivalent (redrawn from the lecture slides)

The two forms are equivalent (Thevenin ↔ Norton), but one is often more convenient depending on the device: a series voltage source suits a resistor, while a diode's current-driven physics often makes a current source the more natural choice.

3. Multi-terminal devices: the input-referred noise model

A 3- or 4-terminal (2-port) device, such as a transistor or an op-amp, can't be modeled with a single noise source. A BJT alone has separate noise contributions from its base, base–emitter junction and collector–emitter junction, and every device topology places them differently. Instead of tracking each source individually, all the noise sources inside the 2-port device are combined into one equivalent series voltage source and one parallel current source at the input. This is the input-referred noise model, and it lets noise calculations be done without knowing what is actually inside the device.

4. Quantifying noise: RMS, mean, variance and PSD

Noise voltage and current have zero mean by definition, so the quantity that matters is the noise power, found through the RMS value. Real systems only pass a limited band of frequencies, so noise power also needs a description per frequency: the power spectral density (PSD). The PSD is the average noise power measured through a very narrow bandpass filter centered at each frequency, normalized per Hz of bandwidth.

Total noise power in a band [fL, fH] Ptotal = ∫fLfH S(f) df  |  flat (white) PSD: P = S·(fH−fL) = S·B

This is why Part 2's thermal-noise formulas multiply a flat PSD by a bandwidth Δf to get a power: it is this integral, simplified for white noise.

5. When can noise powers simply add?

Not always. Writing the instantaneous power of two combined noise voltages across a resistance R and averaging over time gives a cross term in addition to the two individual power terms:

Average combined noise power PAVG = P1,AVG + P2,AVG + 2T∫v1(t)v2(t) dt

That cross term goes to zero, leaving the simple PAVG = P1,AVG+P2,AVG rule used throughout Part 2, only when the two sources are independent (their instantaneous product averages to zero over time). When the sources are correlated, the cross term remains and the simple sum underestimates the true noise power.

Real-world example: two amplifiers powered from the same noisy supply rail each pick up that rail's ripple noise. Their own intrinsic noise sources are independent of each other, but both outputs share the same ripple term (scaled by each amplifier's PSRR). That shared part is correlated, so it can't be summed as an independent power term the way each amplifier's intrinsic noise can.

6. Noise through a frequency-dependent system

A linear system with transfer function H(f) scales a noise PSD at its input by the squared magnitude of the transfer function:

Output PSD through a linear system Sout(f) = |H(f)|2·Sin(f)

Worked example: thermal noise from a resistor R (white PSD Sin) filtered by its own RC low-pass network, |H(f)|² = 11+(2πfRC)².

Pout,AVG = ∫0∞ Sin1+(2πfRC)² df = Sin·π2RC

Rewriting in terms of the filter's actual −3 dB cutoff f−3 = 12πRC:

Pout,AVG = Sin × π2·f−3

So a real (rounded-off) filter passes exactly as much noise power as an ideal "brick-wall" filter whose width, the effective noise bandwidth, is π/2 times the filter's own −3 dB cutoff frequency, not the cutoff frequency itself. Because of this equal-area argument, a filter's noise bandwidth is always somewhat wider than its 3 dB bandwidth.

7. Noise figure: comparing against a standard reference

To judge how much noise a system itself adds, separately from any noise its input signal already carries, every source resistance is assigned a standardized reference noise, using a fixed reference temperature T0 = 290 K (the maximum available thermal noise power a source resistance can deliver, by the maximum power transfer theorem):

Standardized reference noise & noise factor PN,ref = kBT0Δf  |  F = 1 + excess input‑referred noise powerkBT0Δf

The noise factor F is independent of the input signal itself and depends only on the system's intrinsic noise characteristics, which makes it a fair way to compare the noise performance of two different systems. Expressed in decibels, NF = 10log10(F).
