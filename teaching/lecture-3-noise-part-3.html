Parts 1–2 introduced noise sources and their spectral densities. This lecture shows how to build circuit-level noise models of real semiconductor devices: first the diode, then the bipolar junction transistor (BJT). Both are non-linear, so the analysis starts from a small-signal (linearised) model and attaches the physical noise mechanisms to it. It ends with the more subtle point: why a BJT’s input-referred noise needs a voltage and a current source, and why the two cannot be treated as independent.

1. Small-signal model of a diode

A diode is a non-linear device described by the Shockley equation:

Shockley diode equation ID = IS(eVDηVT − 1),   VT = kBTq ≈ 25.7 mV at room temperature

with IS the reverse saturation current (order of 10−10 A), η the ideality factor (typically 1–2) and VT the thermal voltage. The equation assumes steady state, so it says nothing about how the diode responds to time-varying signals.

We study small signals because they are the ones most susceptible to noise. For a small signal the diode is usually forward biased, and in the small region around the operating point Q the curve can be replaced by its tangent:

Slope at the Q-point dIDdVD |Q = IDQ + ISηVT ≈ IDQηVT   (since IS ≈ 10−10 A ≪ IDQ)

This slope has units of conductance, so its inverse is a resistance, called the dynamic resistance rd = ηVTIDQ. To capture time-varying behaviour the junction capacitance Cj and the diffusion capacitance Cd are placed in parallel with rd.

2. Diode noise sources

A diode has three main noise sources, all attached to the small-signal model:

  • Shot noise: the PN junction is a potential barrier that carriers must cross, so the diode current has shot noise, modelled as a parallel current source across the junction (hence across rd): īns2 = 2qIDQΔf, with IDQ the quiescent current.
  • Flicker noise: as a semiconductor device, it has 1/f noise, also a parallel current source: īnF2 = KF IDQα ln(fHfL), where KF and α are device-dependent constants.
  • Thermal noise: the contacts and silicon substrate have a real resistance Rs, modelled as a resistor in series with rd that generates a series voltage noise v̄nRs2 = 4kBT RsΔf.
Fig. 1: Small-signal noise model of a diode (redrawn from the lecture slides)
Why doesn’t rd make thermal noise? The dynamic resistance is not a real resistor. It is a behavioural quantity added to the model to match the diode’s I–V relationship. Only physical resistances (like the bulk resistance Rs) generate thermal noise.

3. Total diode noise voltage

The three sources sit in different branches, so they cannot simply be added. Rs’s thermal noise is a series voltage, whereas shot and flicker noise are parallel currents. The fix is to convert the parallel currents into an equivalent series (Thévenin) voltage across rd, after which KVL applies and, with the sources uncorrelated, the mean-square values add:

Total noise voltage of a diode (with Δf = fH − fL) v̄n,tot2 = 4kBTΔf Rs + [ 2qIDQΔf + KF IDQα ln(fHfL) ] rd2

Substituting IDQ = ηVTrd and VT = kBTq collapses the shot-noise term and gives the compact form from the lecture:

v̄n,tot2 = 2kBTΔf (2Rs + ηrd) + KF IDQα ln(fHfL) rd2

For simplicity the capacitances are ignored here (open circuit), which is reasonable for frequencies up to the MHz range where their roll-off has not yet begun.

4. BJT small-signal model and current gain

There are several small-signal models for a BJT. The lecture groups them into physics-based models (the hybrid-π model, with simplified, low-frequency and high-frequency variants, and the T-model) and two-port / black-box models (h-parameters, built from measured terminal behaviour). The analysis uses the high-frequency hybrid-π model with gm, rπ, ro, Cπ and Cμ (Fig. 2).

Linearising IC ≈ ISeVBEVT about the operating point gives the transconductance gm = ICQVT (collector shorted so ro does not matter), and the DC current gain follows directly:

DC current gain βDC = ICIB = gm rπ

With the collector shorted for AC, the base sees rπ in parallel with Cπ and Cμ, so the AC current gain is a low-pass response:

AC current gain and unity-gain frequency βAC = βDC1 + jωrπ(Cπ+Cμ)
|βAC|2 = 11βDC2 + (ωωT)2
ωT = gmCπ+Cμ

This resembles an RC low-pass filter with corner f−3 = 12πrπ(Cπ+Cμ). As ω approaches ωT the current gain falls to 1: beyond that the BJT no longer works as an amplifier, so ωT is the maximum useful operating frequency. Because gm = ICQ/VT, the unity-gain frequency is not a fixed property of the device. It changes with the quiescent collector current.

5. The six noise sources of a BJT

A BJT has six main noise sources: three thermal, two shot and one flicker.

  • Thermal noise from the bulk resistances of the emitter, base and collector regions (RE, RB, RC), added in series with the corresponding terminals. RE and RC are generally ignored: the emitter is heavily doped (minimal resistance), and the collector, although lightly doped, has a wide cross-section and a well-doped substrate to carry signals outward. RB cannot be ignored: the base is a very narrow, only moderately doped region, so its resistance is comparatively high, and noise generated there is amplified by β.
  • Shot noise: there are two current paths, and each crosses a potential barrier. Forward-biasing the base–emitter junction sends electrons from the highly doped emitter into the thin base; almost all diffuse across and are swept into the collector, so the collector current is a stream of carriers crossing a barrier. This gives collector shot noise īnC2 = 2qICQΔf, in parallel with the C–E junction. The small difference between IE and IC is the base current IB (a few charges recombine in the base); it crosses the B–E junction and gives base shot noise īnB2 = 2qIBQΔf, in parallel with the B–E junction.
  • Flicker noise: as with any semiconductor, trap states give 1/f noise, modelled as a current source in parallel with the B–E junction: īnF2 = K IBQα ln(fHfL).
Fig. 2: Hybrid-π model of a BJT with its noise sources (redrawn from the lecture slides). RE and RC are neglected.

As with the diode, the dynamic resistances rπ and ro are not real resistors and add no noise.

6. Referring BJT noise to the input

A BJT is a two-port, so its equivalent input-referred noise model needs a series voltage source and a parallel current source across the input port. Everything is transferred to the input side: base thermal noise is already in series at the input; base shot and flicker noise are already across the input; only the collector shot noise is still at the output.

To refer the collector shot noise to the input as a current, divide by the squared magnitude of the AC current gain; to refer it as a voltage, divide by the squared transconductance (an AC quantity):

Collector shot noise referred to the input īnc−ir2 = īnC2|βAC|2 = 2qΔf IBQ 1 + (ωrπ(Cπ+Cμ))2gm rπ
v̄nc−ir2 = īnC2gm2 = 2qΔf IBQ rπgm

Adding up the pieces, the complete input-referred noise model of the BJT is:

Current noise īni2 = īnB2 + īnc−ir2 + īnF2 = 2qIBQΔf  [1 + 1 + (ωrπ(Cπ+Cμ))2gm rπ] + K IBQα ln(fHfL) Voltage noise v̄ni2 = v̄nrB2 + v̄nc−ir2 = 4kBTΔf rb + 2qΔf IBQ rπgm

The additions above are valid only when the sources have zero mean and are uncorrelated, and when KVL/KCL permit the addition. In reality the collector and base noise are somewhat correlated, but for this course they are treated as uncorrelated.

7. Why both a voltage and a current source are needed

This is the most important concept in the lecture. The input-referred collector shot noise appears twice in the model, as īnc−ir and as v̄nc−ir, but they come from the same noise source, so they are not independent. When the total noise at the output is calculated, the contributions of these two terms cannot simply be added as squared quantities, because their correlation matters.

The calculation uses superposition for each source with the signal source replaced by its source resistance RS (at low frequency, so Cπ and Cμ drop out). The result for the output noise current of a common-emitter stage is:

Output noise current including source resistance RS īout2 = (gm rπRB+RS+rπ)2 × [ v̄nB2 + v̄nc−ir2 + (īnB2+īnF2)(RB+RS)2 + RS2 īnc−ir2 + 2RS v̄nc−ir2 īnc−ir2 ]

The last term is the cross-correlation between the voltage and current forms of the collector shot noise. Two observations follow:

  • Ideal source (RS = 0): the expression collapses to īout2 ≈ gm2(v̄nB2 + v̄nc−ir2), and the base thermal noise and the voltage form of the collector shot noise dominate. The base shot and flicker noise drop out because RB is small.
  • Non-zero source resistance: the current form of the collector shot noise now matters too, because it flows through RS. If only the current-source part were propagated to the output (or only the voltage part), the result would not equal the true collector noise current īnC; with both parts and the correlation term, it does.
The takeaway: this is why two-port theory requires both an equivalent voltage and an equivalent current noise source at the input. A single source cannot give correct answers for every source impedance that might be connected. It also shows how the dominant noise source of a BJT amplifier depends on the source impedance.

8. Exercise and take-homes

Exercise 1 (from the lecture). A BJT amplifier has β = 50, gm = 38 mS and rπ = 1.33 kΩ (1 mA bias), with all capacitors noiseless and short-circuited for AC. Measurements on the amplifier give a noise figure of 3 dB with a source resistance of 50 Ω and 9 dB with 500 Ω; the effective bandwidth is 100 kHz. Find the transistor’s input-referred voltage and current noise spectral densities (v̄n2/Hz and īn2/Hz), assuming both are white.

Important take-homes from the lecture:

  • Noise is fundamentally an AC quantity and a small signal in itself; begin with small-signal models for analysis.
  • Dynamic resistances in small-signal models do not generate noise, because they represent behaviour, not physical resistors. Bulk resistances and terminal resistances are physical and must be considered.
  • The capacitances were mostly ignored during these derivations; think about how including them would change the noise characteristics (effective bandwidth).
  • A diode has thermal, shot and flicker noise. A BJT has several intrinsic noise sources, of which four are important (base thermal, base shot, collector shot, flicker).
  • You must be able to calculate the AC current gain βAC and explain why it is needed; refer noise sources to the input so a generic noise model results; and understand why the 2-port model needs both a voltage and a current source at the input.
  • Explain how source impedance changes which noise sources (voltage or current) dominate; use superposition to propagate individual sources, and recognise when input-referred sources are not independent.
  • Derivations are not examinable in detail, but the concepts used (and why each simplification is valid) are.
