Reverse-Engineering a Server PSU into a High-Current BMW Charger

Overview

I picked up a Dell/Delta D1100E-S0 (DPS-1100BB) server power supply: 1100 W, 80 PLUS Platinum, a regulated 12.2 V at up to 90 A. Used ones go for about $30 on eBay, which is nothing compared to a bench supply with that kind of current rating.

I wanted a high-current supply to keep my BMW's battery up while flashing its ECUs. Flashing takes a while, and if the battery voltage sags partway through, the flash can fail and brick the ECUs. This requirement needed two changes to the server PSU:

  1. Getting the main output to turn on without a server motherboard.
  2. Raising the voltage from 12.2 V to the ~13.8–14 V an alternator would normally supply.

There are guides online for doing this to HP server supplies, but this Dell/Delta unit is quite different and none of them match its pinout or feedback circuit. There's no datasheet either, so everything below came from a multimeter, an oscilloscope and a lot of continuity testing.

Step 1: Turning the Power Supply On

Plugging it in wasn't enough. Server PSUs wait for the motherboard to assert a few control signals on a header before they enable the main output, so plugging it in only lit the status LED green:

The main +12 V output was still off at this point, reading about 1.6 mV.

The large terminal on the edge is the main +12 V output, and the terminal in the center is ground. What was already live at this stage was standby power, available from a smaller terminal in the center when the unit is flipped over. It read 12 V but could only supply about 3 A, which was insufficient for the intended application.

Enabling the main output meant working out the control header. It's a 24-pin connector, twelve pins on each side.

Mapping the Control Pins

I began by soldering dedicated ground and power leads directly onto the output copper as I knew I'd need an easily accessible ground line and +12 V tap points later during this analysis. The soldered wires were small gauge for now.

Tip: use flux on the big copper output pads. They soak up heat and are usually a bit oxidized, so solder won't wet them easily without it.

With no datasheet, I had to reverse-engineer the header pin by pin. First, with a multimeter in continuity mode, I found which pins are grounded by default: pins 6 and 10 on the top side, and pins 16, 18, 20, and 22 on the flip side.

Step 2: Raising the Output Voltage

With the supply turning on reliably, the next problem was voltage: I needed roughly 13.8–14 V, and the supply is hard-regulated to 12.2 V. My first approach followed the same trick used on similar HP supplies: exploit the remote-sense input.

Pins 23 and 24 turned out to be Remote Sense (+) and Remote Sense (−). With remote sensing, the supply measures voltage at the load instead of at its own terminals, to make up for drop in the cables. If you feed the sense pin a voltage lower than what's actually present at the output, the control loop "thinks" the output has sagged and compensates by cranking up the real output voltage.

Since the remote-sense (+) pin already sat at the full 12.2 V, it had to be pulled up to the output through some internal resistance. Grounding it through an external resistor would form a voltage divider with that internal resistance, pulling the sensed voltage down and, in theory, pushing the real output up. I tried a range of resistor values from the sense pin to ground and logged the resulting output voltage:

The output voltage plateaued at 12.67 V no matter how low I pushed the external resistance. That's a gain of only about 0.3 V over the open-circuit reading, far short of the 13.8 V+ I needed. A plateau like that points to a fixed internal resistance in series with my external one. Even at 0 Ω external, the internal resistance keeps the sensed voltage above a floor. So, on this supply the remote-sense trick topped out there.

Opening the Case and Tracing the Feedback Network

With remote sense maxed out, I had to find the real feedback network inside and change it. So I opened the case.

Safety note: the primary-side bulk capacitor in a supply like this can hold a lethal charge well after it's unplugged. Supplies like this have active power-factor correction, which keeps that capacitor at roughly 380–400 V regardless of whether the mains input is 120 or 230 VAC. Let it discharge on its own, or discharge it manually, before opening anything up.

After I removed all the case screws, the outer shell came off, and I separated the main board from the housing:

With everything exposed, I taped over the dangling wires and terminals so nothing would short while I probed around.

Here's the layout and the main ICs, starting with the back of the main board. It's a double-sided PCB with two daughterboards on top, a lot more complicated than the single-board HP supplies in other guides. I noticed two microcontrollers, one on a daughterboard, and another on the main PCB.

I started my analysis by tracing pin 23 (remote sense +) to see what it connects to.

It led to two resistors: one from remote sense (+) to the 12 V output, and one to a pin on a comparator IC with no markings (so no datasheet). Since most of the logic on this board runs at 3.3 V, there had to be a third resistor tying that comparator input down to ground, forming a divider that scales the 12 V output down before it's sampled. Probing around confirmed the divider, sketched below.

Without knowing what the IC was, I couldn't get any further this way. I turned my attention to the over-voltage-protection circuit instead, on the thought that the supply might be hitting an OVP clamp that was independently limiting the output. That circuit uses an LM393 dual comparator, but next to it was a transistor-looking part marked only “64” that I couldn't identify either. It wasn't a BJT: a diode test didn't show the two junctions a BJT would have.

I dropped that idea: if the supply were actually hitting an OVP fault at 12.7 V, I'd expect to see a corresponding fault code on the PMBus (I did connect to the PMBus during this project, but lost the logged data, so I'm not including it here). More importantly, even if this LM393 circuit was what was clamping the output when I adjusted the remote-sense network, its output would still have to feed back into one of the two on-board microcontrollers, and judging by their location and the parts around them, one handles the AC input and down-conversion while the other handles output voltage feedback. Since the microcontroller on the daughterboard sits physically closest to the output circuitry, I assumed it was the one responsible for voltage regulation, and shifted my search there.

Finding the Feedback Pin on the Microcontroller

The main daughterboard is built around a dsPIC33FJ64GS606. I couldn't find a direct continuity path from the 12 V output to the daughterboard, so something (a buffer, a resistor) had to be in between. Instead I powered it up and carefully probed for a point near 12 V.

I pulled the microcontroller's datasheet to locate its ADC-capable pins:

Microchip dsPIC33FJ64GS606 datasheet (PDF)

Then I probed every ADC pin, looking for anything between 0 and 3.3 V that could be the feedback input. I had a specific target in mind: I'd read that this supply family has an over-voltage cutoff around 14.5 V, and if that's true, whatever ADC samples the output should read 3.3 V exactly at that 14.5 V cutoff. At the nominal 12.2 V output, that same ADC should read (12.2 / 14.5) × 3.3 V ≈ 2.77 V, so that's the value I was hunting for.

One pin read about 2.7 V. This pin was also connected to a test point, making it easy to probe.

To confirm it, I repeated the remote-sense-resistor experiment while watching this pin directly. Grounding the remote-sense pin through a 5 kΩ resistor only pulled this candidate pin down to about 2.6 V, and a 570 Ω resistor pulled it down to about 2 V. That's about what you'd expect from a real feedback node.

The test point adjacent to it tracked the 12 V output directly.

When I added the remote-sense resistor and the output went up, this voltage went up with it, so both are part of the same feedback path.

Tracing the Resistive Dividers

To actually change what the microcontroller reads as feedback, I needed to find the resistive divider(s) that scale the 12 V output down into the 3.3 V range the ADC expects, and modify them.

I traced one end of a resistor (silkscreen-labeled 07C) back to the 12 V rail. The other end of 07C connects to a resistor (50B) with a capacitor in parallel, whose far sides both go to ground. That's the lower leg of a divider. The midpoint of this divider feeds the tied-together PIC pins 14 and 15, both of which are ADC-capable pins. I labeled this as "network 1".

Right next to it, a second resistor (labeled 54C) also connects to the 12 V rail, and its other end feeds into another resistor (02C) with a parallel capacitor, again returning to ground. So there's a second, independent divider. The midpoint of this second divider feeds PIC pin 13, also an ADC-capable pin. I labeled this as "network 2".

Both dividers sense the output and have nearly the same ratio, about 0.22. That puts each midpoint at 12.2 × 0.22 ≈ 2.68 V, which matches what I'd measured.

Building the Trim Circuit

Earlier, I'd seen that adding external resistance at the remote-sense pin and raising the real output voltage caused the voltage at the resistive dividers to drop. So to make the microcontroller regulate higher, I needed to lower the voltage at the divider midpoints, which meant lowering the resistance of each divider's bottom leg. The easiest way was to add a resistor in parallel with each bottom-leg resistor.

Conveniently, there were already exposed test points at both divider midpoints, so I soldered fine tie-wire onto each one to bring the nodes out to somewhere I could work with them.

I kept the stripped ends short as a long bare end can short to a nearby part. Also, a long wire can pick up noise and can affect the readings on this sensitive ADC input.

Bringing the nodes out this way enabled me to try a range of parallel resistor values rather than committing to one. I worked out the target resistor values with a quick set of divider calculations. I've labeled the modified version of "network 1" as "network 3", and the modified version of "network 2" as "network 4":

Picking the pot's range mattered. The divider ratio changes fast at low resistance and flattens out as resistance goes up (see the two graphs below). With a very large pot, almost all the adjustment would happen in the first few turns and the rest would do almost nothing. So I capped the maximum resistance: high enough to get back close to the stock ratio at full scale, low enough that the adjustment is spread more evenly across the turns.

A 50 kΩ pot was enough for the first divider. At full resistance the ratio is about 0.21, close to the stock 0.22, so it barely changes anything at that end of its range. Likewise, a 100 kΩ variable resistor was sufficient for the second divider, again landing around a 0.21 ratio at maximum resistance.

Dialing In the Voltage

With a variable resistor wired onto the first divider, the output climbed to 13.2 V:

Turning the pot further counterclockwise pushed the voltage higher still:

When I went too far, the over-voltage protection tripped. The status LED started blinking yellow (fault mode), and my on/off switch couldn't clear it, so the supply needed a full power cycle to reset.

Adjusting only one of the two dividers, I could reach at most about 13.85 V before triggering OVP.

Adjusting both dividers together pushed the ceiling higher, since neither ADC input alone reached the microcontroller's trip threshold as quickly. I could get to about 14.2 V this way.

Instead of leaving two loose trimmer pots hanging inside, I made a small board with one pot per divider so I can adjust the voltage after the case is closed.

Load Testing

After reassembling the case, I soldered a pair of low-gauge (large-diameter) wires directly to the main output to handle real current, and set up a load test with a high-current DC motor.

For the load, I used a blower motor from a 2014 BMW 750i with seized bearings, which draws a lot of current. That made it a decent stand-in for a real car load.

Current was measured with a clamp ammeter, which had a zero error of −2.6 A that needed to be accounted for in the readings.

With the trim pots set to bring the output to 13.91 V, I powered the motor:

With the motor running, the clamp ammeter read 17.8 A, which, correcting for the −2.6 A zero error, works out to about 20.4 A actually flowing into the motor. Under that load the output dropped from 13.91 V to about 13.75 V, only 0.16 V, which is fine for this.

It held nearly 14 V at over 20 A, so it did the job: it stands in for the alternator and keeps the battery up while I flash the BMW's ECUs.
