Quick Facts

  • The failure: 
    The factory temperature knob on older Lancers relies on a cable-and-lever mechanism to pull the blend door. The linkage stiffens with age, and eventually the knob snaps before the door moves. New knobs break the same way because the problem is the linkage being insufficient to turn a stiffened internal mechanism.
  • The fix: 
    I bypassed the cable mechanism entirely, using a 20 kg‑cm servo to drive the blend-door gear directly and a potentiometer on the dashboard knob to tell it where to go. I designed the servo mount so it needed no holes in the factory actuator.
  • Actuator:
    I picked a Miuzei 20 kg‑cm digital servo over a stepper (open loop, needs a driver, bigger for the same torque) and a DC gear motor (needs its own PID loop to hold position).
  • Position sensing:
    I read a 10 kΩ potentiometer through an RC low-pass filter and mapped it through a hand-calibrated lookup table instead of a plain map().
  • Control boards:
    I used an Arduino Nano to run the servo/potentiometer loop for the basic fix, and built a different board pairing an ESP8266 NodeMCU with a DRV8833 dual H-bridge for a motorized-knob upgrade.
  • Power: 
    I tapped the cigarette lighter's 12 V and stepped it down through a Tobsun EA50-5V 50 W (10 A) buck converter, which I sized for the servo's ~3 A stall current plus headroom for the knob motor and a Wi-Fi‑enabled board.
  • Fabrication:
    I 3D printed all the brackets, housings, couplers and the enclosure. I measured the factory mounting holes with calipers and a clay imprint, since there's no CAD model or open-source dimensions for this part.

The Problem: The Knob Keeps Breaking

The temperature knob on older Lancers is known for getting so stiff that it snaps. Mine did.

I replaced it and it broke again. Therefore, the knob wasn't the real problem. Behind the dashboard, a cable-pull mechanism uses a set of gears and levers to amplify the knob's motion and pull the blend door across its range. As it stiffens, the gears and levers can't overcome the resistance anymore, and the force ends up snapping the knob instead of moving the door.

Quick Mechanical-Advantage Check

Before rebuilding anything, I wanted a rough number for why the linkage was failing. I modeled the mechanism as two rigid bodies meeting at a CAM contact: body a, which receives the pulling force from the knob's cable, and body b, which delivers the output force into the blend-door's bevel gear. A cylindrical boss on body b rides in a track on body a, so the two only ever interact through that single contact point.

Balancing torque about Fulcruma gives the torque body a carries into the CAM contact:

τa = Fin sin(θ(a1,Fin)) × da1

The tangential force at the contact is F′ = τada2, and resolving that along the actual contact normal gives the CAM reaction force. Since the contact is assumed frictionless, that same magnitude pushes back on body b (Newton's third law):

FRcam(b|a) = FRcam(a|b) = Fin sin(θ(a1,Fin)) × da1cos(θRcama) × da2

On body b, that reaction creates a torque about Fulcrumb, which must balance the torque the output force applies at arm db2:

FRcam(b|a) cos(θRcamb) × db1 = Fout sin(θ(b2,Fout)) × db2

Substituting the reaction force from the first half of the derivation and solving for the ratio the linkage actually delivers:

FoutFin = cos(θRcamb) × sin(θ(a1,Fin)) × da1 × db1cos(θRcama) × sin(θ(b2,Fout)) × da2 × db2

Not all of these arms and angles are fixed, though. As the knob turns, the CAM boss slides along body a's track, so da2 and every angle in the formula changes continuously over the knob's travel:

To put numbers in, I took rough measurements of the real linkage, using the least favourable arm ratios and generous values for the angles:

  • The ratio da1/da2 bottoms out around 1/1.5 at the CAM's far travel limit.
  • The fixed ratio db1/db2 is also about 1/1.5.
  • The cable keeps θ(a1,Fin) close enough to 90° through its small sweep that sin(θ(a1,Fin)) ≈ 1.
  • The output angle θ(b2,Fout) swings between 60° and 120°, bottoming out at sin(θ(b2,Fout)) = sin(60°) = √3/2 ≈ 0.87 at either extreme.
  • Giving the geometry the benefit of the doubt, cos(θRcamb)/cos(θRcama) ≈ 1.5.

Plugging all of that in:

FoutFin ≈ 1.5 × 2√3 × 11.5 × 11.5 ≈ 0.77

So even with generous assumptions, the linkage doesn't amplify force. It loses about 23%, trading force for knob travel. That matched what happened in the car: with no force margin, a slightly stiffer mechanism was enough for the knob to break first.

A new knob wasn't going to fix the linkage, so I decided to motorize the temperature control. That also opened the door to automatic climate control later.

Choosing How to Actuate the Blend Door

Three options were on the table: a DC gear motor with an encoder, a stepper motor, or a servo.

I picked the servo (a Miuzei 20 kg‑cm digital unit) because it has a lot of torque for its size and positions repeatably.

A stepper with the same torque would be bigger, need a driver IC, and run open loop, so with a gear this stiff I'd have no way of knowing whether it actually moved. A DC gear motor would also be bigger and would need its own PID loop to hold a position. The servo already has closed-loop positioning built in.

Reverse-Engineering the Mounting Points

After I removed the lever mechanism under the dashboard, several mounting holes on the AC blend door actuator itself were exposed. These were the holes the factory linkage used, and the only real option for anchoring a bracket.

My plan was to design a bracket that picked up those same holes, with the servo's output shaft mating to the temperature blend door's gear coupler. The diameters were easy to measure with calipers, but the positions weren't: the holes sat at different depths in a cramped spot, and I couldn't get the caliper in at a consistent angle.

So I pressed a thick layer of modeling clay on a hard backing against the holes to get an imprint, then measured the imprint on the bench.

I didn't want to waste a full bracket print on bad measurements, so first I printed a bare skeleton with a pin at each hole location. It also showed how uneven the hole depths were:

After a few rounds of this, the positions and depths were right.

Designing and Printing the Servo Bracket

In my first real attempts, I used a large rectangular piece to hold the servo, with a separate circular piece keying into a subset of the available actuator holes. I designed these in SolidWorks and 3D printed them in PLA on a Creality Ender 3 V2:

With only one anchor point, the bracket bent as the servo fought the stiff gear, so it failed. I then built a two-point design that resists the twisting as the servo drives the gear. I split the design into multiple pieces, each with a flat face on the build plate, so they would stick well and need no supports. That way, if one piece broke or needed a change, I only had to reprint that piece.

Servos come with plastic and metal output horns that mate tightly with the splines on the output shaft. I trimmed one of these to shape, friction-fit it onto the shaft, then glued the original gear onto it with JB-Weld two-part epoxy. It has held up for 3 years so far! Lining up the servo shaft with the gear's axis was critical. Any misalignment here could damage the bracket due to undue stress, hinder free mobility of the servo or break the glue bond.

Closing the Loop: Sensing the Knob Position

Measuring the Knob Position

The servo also needed to know where the dashboard knob was set. In my first attempt, I tapped the cable that already ran under the dashboard and fed it into a custom potentiometer. For that, I 3D printed a housing for an angular potentiometer built from copper pads at each detent position. However, it didn't produce stable enough readings, so next I tried a commercial linear potentiometer, again with a printed housing. That didn't work as expected either.

Therefore I took the cable mechanism out completely and mounted a plain rotary potentiometer at the dashboard controller, held with a nut. While the nut held the potentiometer in place, it could get loosened due to repeated rotation. Therefore, I glued the potentiometer as an additional structural safety measure. I used hot glue temporarily because it was easy to undo.

Coupling the Knob to the Potentiometer

With the cable-pull mechanism gone, the dashboard knob itself needed to couple directly to the potentiometer's shaft. I modeled the original knob shaft (view in 3D) in SolidWorks, but its shape didn't fit the potentiometer, so I designed and printed a coupler (view in 3D).

Print orientation mattered here. For accuracy, the part had to be printed with the shaft axis vertical. However, this left the layers weak against twisting. Printing on its side would give a part that is much stronger against torsion, but less accurate, since there's no flat face for the build plate. I went with accuracy, but found that it created a lot of issues downstream: the PLA coupler and the knob shaft both deformed in the summer heat inside the car. While using ABS material instead of PLA may have solved this, my printer can't do ABS reliably. Therefore, I added a hole through the center for a metal dowel pin, which reinforces the weak layers, preventing bending or deforming. Note that a real dowel pin is not necessary here: a nail with the head cut off, or a thick safety pin bent back and forth until it breaks to length, works fine.

The Control Board

I built a custom PCB to connect an Arduino Nano (or an ESP8266 NodeMCU for wireless control) to the potentiometer and servo.

I also added headers for a Bluetooth module, buttons and an OLED screen for later, but they are irrelevant to this project.

I took power from the cigarette lighter's 12 V, so a fault here couldn't take anything else in the car down with it, and used a Tobsun EA50-5V 50 W buck converter to step it down to 5 V efficiently, with plenty of current headroom:

It's rated well above what this build alone draws: the servo's stall current is about 3 A, the motorized-knob motor (discussed later) draws about 1 A at stall, and a Wi-Fi‑enabled board can briefly pull ~1 A while establishing a connection. That's about 5 A worst case, well under the converter's 10 A rating.

I found that the ground connection at the cigarette lighter is unstable and introduces noise to the circuit in the form of ground fluctuations. Therefore, I used a stronger ground: attaching the ground to a screw that screws directly onto the chassis, under the center arm rest.

Installing It in the Car

I put the board and wiring in a 3D-printed enclosure in the cabin. I gave it one opening for a micro/mini-USB cable, so the Arduino can be reprogrammed without disassembling anything, and a second opening for the wiring harness.

I ran the wiring under the dashboard, wrapped in harness covering. I used lever-nut connectors for joints that need higher current capacity and might need servicing later, JST connectors for signal lines, and crimp connectors anywhere the joint was meant to be permanent.

Signal Conditioning and the Lookup Table

Rather than use Arduino's built-in map() function on the raw potentiometer reading, I used a lookup table for three reasons:

  1. The knob only has a few discrete positions, so a continuous mapping isn't needed.
  2. The potentiometer I had was noisy, and the reading drifted enough to make the servo jitter. The table's fixed bins absorb that drift (software hysteresis would also work).
  3. The potentiometer itself was non-linear, with the resistance changing slowly at the low end and quickly at the high end.

I built a calibration table by sweeping the potentiometer through equal angular steps and recording the ADC value at each one. It maps those uneven raw values onto evenly spaced servo angles.

I added a hardware RC low-pass filter between the potentiometer output and the ADC input to further cut noise from resistance variation. I wanted the filter to settle in under 100 ms. A filter needs roughly 5 time constants (5×R×C) to fully settle, and the maximum Thevenin-equivalent resistance the filter capacitor ever sees is about 2.5 kΩ (5 kΩ in parallel with 5 kΩ, at the midpoint of the 10 kΩ potentiometer). The true worst case for a non-linear pot may not fall exactly at the midpoint, but this gives an upper limit on the capacitor of about 8 µF:

5 × 2.5kΩ × C < 100 ms  ⇒  C < 8 µF

I chose a 4.7 µF capacitor, giving a low-pass cutoff of roughly 12πRmaxC ≈ 13.5 Hz.

Here's the calibrated lookup-table firmware for the basic servo-only version. It averages ten ADC samples per reading, then maps the result through the calibration bins:

// As explained in the text/video, I have used two microcontroller boards here for two separate builds, one an Arduino Nano, and the second, an ESP8266 NodeMCU.
// To run code on an Arduino Nano:
//   1. Use plain pin numbers for digital pins. The Nano has no "D7" name, so write
//      #define servo_signal_pin 7 (or whichever pin the servo is on).
// However, A0 doesn't have to be changed.
//   2. Change Serial.begin(74880) to Serial.begin(9600) and set the Serial Monitor to match.
//      (74880 is the ESP8266's boot baud rate, so its startup messages stay readable.)
//   3. Redo the calibration (lookup table). Both ADCs read 0-1023, but the Nano has a full scale voltage 0-5 V and the NodeMCU only has 0-3.3 V, so the bin edges in ac_pot_trig_vals will shift.

#include <Arduino.h>
#include <Servo.h>

#define servo_signal_pin D7  // The pin that sends the PWM signals to the servo.
#define pot_input_pin A0     // The pin that reads the potentiometer output voltage - Must be an ADC linkable pin.

int current_pot_val;  // Stores the last received potentiometer value

// Below we implement a lookup-table type logic to 1) reduce effects of noise causing servo jitter, 2) workaround the non-linearity in my potentiometer.
// Note: If your potentiometer is a linear one, the following manual mapping would become equal spaced.
const int num_ac_steps = 11;  // The number of virtual detents/levels of temperatures
int ac_pot_trig_vals[num_ac_steps + 1] = {1024, 930, 910, 890, 870, 850, 760, 670, 580, 490, 450, 0};  // AC detent bin edges for the potentiometer values.
int ac_servo_angle_vals[num_ac_steps] = {0, 18, 36, 54, 72, 90, 108, 126, 144, 162, 180};  // Corresponding servo angles
int ac_servo_step_delay_ms = 50;  // Low delay = quick response but louder servo noise.
int ac_servo_angle = 0;
Servo acServoObj;

// Poll, average, and extract the potentiometer reading.
int getACKnobPotValue(){
  int num_reps = 10;
  current_pot_val = 0;
  for (int i=0; i<num_reps; i++){
    current_pot_val += analogRead(pot_input_pin);
    delayMicroseconds(10);
  }
  current_pot_val /= num_reps;
  return current_pot_val;
}

// Walk the lookup table to find which bin the potentiometer value lands in.
int getServoAngleBasedOnPotVal(){
  current_pot_val = getACKnobPotValue();
  for (int i=0; i<num_ac_steps; i++){
    if ((current_pot_val<ac_pot_trig_vals[i]) && (current_pot_val >= ac_pot_trig_vals[i+1])){
      ac_servo_angle = ac_servo_angle_vals[i];
      break;
    }
  }
  return ac_servo_angle;
}

// Poll the potentiometer, get the target angle, and step the servo to it.
void updateServoPosition() {
  int current_ac_servo_angle = ac_servo_angle;
  int target_ac_servo_angle = getServoAngleBasedOnPotVal();

  if (current_ac_servo_angle < target_ac_servo_angle) {
    for (int pos = current_ac_servo_angle; pos <= target_ac_servo_angle; pos++) {
      acServoObj.write(pos);
      delay(ac_servo_step_delay_ms);
    }
  } else {
    for (int pos = current_ac_servo_angle; pos >= target_ac_servo_angle; pos--) {
      acServoObj.write(pos);
      delay(ac_servo_step_delay_ms);
    }
  }
  Serial.print("Current pot val: ");
  Serial.print(current_pot_val);
  Serial.print(", Current ac servo angle: ");
  Serial.println(ac_servo_angle);
}

void setup() {
  Serial.begin(74880);
  acServoObj.attach(servo_signal_pin);
  pinMode(pot_input_pin, INPUT);
}

void loop() {
  updateServoPosition();
  delay(100);
}

Automating the Knob Itself

The long-term plan is a temperature sensor and PID loop that set the blend door automatically, i.e., automatic climate control. But then the dashboard knob would stop matching what the system is actually doing, since it is not being physically turned.

So I made the knob turn itself, using a motorized potentiometer salvaged from a broken Sony radio.

This part did need cutting and drilling into the dashboard-side actuator body, the one part of this build that wasn't fully reversible.

With the motorized potentiometer in place, the knob can now be driven to any position on command. I wrote the firmware to accept single-character serial commands and dispatch them to the matching function. Sending H, for instance, calls heatCar(). A DRV8833CPWR (dual H-bridge, 3–10 V, 1.5 A) drives the knob motor, which is plenty for its current draw. I built a different custom PCB to house the driver alongside an ESP8266 NodeMCU:

For this version, I kept the same calibrated lookup-table servo control as before and added open-loop motor control for the knob itself. It drives the knob toward a target extreme while polling the potentiometer to know when to stop, with a timeout in case the motor stalls against something:

// As explained in the text/video, I have used two microcontroller boards here for two separate builds, one an Arduino Nano, and the second, an ESP8266 NodeMCU. This sketch doesn't use Wi-Fi, so it runs on either.
// To run code on an Arduino Nano:
//   1. Use plain pin numbers for digital pins. The Nano has no "D5"/"D7" names, so the servo and the
//      DRV8833's IN1, IN2 and enable pins (D7, D5, D2, D6) become 7, 5, 2, 6 (or whichever pins you wire).
// However, A0 doesn't have to be changed.
//   2. Change Serial.begin(74880) to Serial.begin(9600) and set the Serial Monitor to match.
//      (74880 is the ESP8266's boot baud rate, so its startup messages stay readable.)
//   3. Redo the calibration (lookup table). Both ADCs read 0-1023, but the Nano has a full scale voltage 0-5 V and the NodeMCU only has 0-3.3 V, so the bin edges in ac_pot_trig_vals and the knob end-stops (ac_pot_max_heat_val, ac_pot_max_cold_val) will shift.

// The following three pins control the direction/activation of the motor driver for the motorized knob
#define control_knob_motor_driver_in_1_pin D5
#define control_knob_motor_driver_in_2_pin D2
#define control_knob_motor_driver_enable_pin D6

long ac_control_knob_turn_timeout_ms = 15000;  // In case the motor can't reach the target (blockage, etc.)
int ac_pot_max_heat_val = 350;
int ac_pot_max_cold_val = 950;

// Compares a character received via serial against presets to trigger heating or cooling.
void handleSerialInputForACKnobControlSystem(char c){
    if (c == 'H') { Serial.println("Heating Car"); heatCar(); }
    else if (c == 'C') { Serial.println("Cooling Car"); coolCar(); }
}

// Turns the motorized knob toward max heat, open loop, until the potentiometer confirms arrival (or timeout).
void heatCar(){
  enableACKnobMotorDriver();
  turnACKnobMotorClockwise();
  long start_time = millis();
  while ((current_pot_val > ac_pot_max_heat_val) && ((millis() - start_time) < ac_control_knob_turn_timeout_ms)){
    current_pot_val = getACKnobPotValue();
    delay(1);
  }
  stopACKnobMotor();
  disableACKnobMotorDriver();
  if (current_pot_val > ac_pot_max_heat_val) {
    Serial.println("WARNING: heatCar() timed out before reaching target position.");
  }
}

// Mirror of heatCar() toward max cool.
void coolCar(){
  enableACKnobMotorDriver();
  turnACKnobMotorCounterClockwise();
  long start_time = millis();
  while ((current_pot_val < ac_pot_max_cold_val) && ((millis() - start_time) < ac_control_knob_turn_timeout_ms)){
    current_pot_val = getACKnobPotValue();
    delay(1);
  }
  stopACKnobMotor();
  disableACKnobMotorDriver();
  if (current_pot_val < ac_pot_max_cold_val) {
    Serial.println("WARNING: coolCar() timed out before reaching target position.");
  }
}

void turnACKnobMotorClockwise() {
  digitalWrite(control_knob_motor_driver_in_1_pin, LOW);
  digitalWrite(control_knob_motor_driver_in_2_pin, HIGH);
}
void turnACKnobMotorCounterClockwise() {
  digitalWrite(control_knob_motor_driver_in_1_pin, HIGH);
  digitalWrite(control_knob_motor_driver_in_2_pin, LOW);
}
void stopACKnobMotor(){
  digitalWrite(control_knob_motor_driver_in_1_pin, HIGH);
  digitalWrite(control_knob_motor_driver_in_2_pin, HIGH);
}
void disableACKnobMotorDriver(){ digitalWrite(control_knob_motor_driver_enable_pin, LOW); }
void enableACKnobMotorDriver(){ digitalWrite(control_knob_motor_driver_enable_pin, HIGH); }

void loop() {
  if (Serial.available() > 0) {
    char c = Serial.read();
    Serial.print("Received Serial Code ");
    Serial.println(c);
    handleSerialInputForACKnobControlSystem(c);
    handleSerialInputForACServo(c);
  }
  updateServoPosition();  // Poll the (now motorized) potentiometer and keep the servo in sync with it.
  delay(100);
}
